# Magic Lantern

When I was younger, I could spend an entire day searching for music.

I'd watch MTV2 and write down the names of bands I hadn't heard before. I'd watch Gonzo and 120 Minutes and make a note of anything that excited me. Then I'd go into Track Records in York looking to find the best stuff on CD.

I'd spend three days waiting for a music video to download from Soulseek or LimeWire.

I remember sitting on the bus with a friend, one earbud each, listening to an album I'd just discovered and had to share with them.

I got a Mohawk because I wanted to look like someone in a band I was obsessed with.

I'd go to gigs, go to clubs, find new records and talk about them with friends.

My parents were always playing music when I was growing up. They'd have people over and I'd sit by the Bose stereo choosing albums and playing tracks from them.

Music gave me a sense of who I was.

It felt like there was a whole world out there waiting to be discovered, and I'd barely scratched the surface.

You could spend hours listening to an album because you'd bought it and there wasn't anything else competing for your attention. You'd listen to the whole thing over and over. You'd read the booklet. You'd look at the photos. You'd learn the words.

At some point I started getting involved in making music, although I didn't really want to be the person doing it.

It was a bit like when I was a kid. I'd rather sit next to someone playing PlayStation 2 and tell them what I thought they should do than pick up the controller myself.

I'd sit with friends who were on the tools in the DAW and do much the same thing, suggesting things to improve the track. I liked being close to the process without having to commit to spending eight hours EQing a kick drum.

Eventually I started writing songs myself.

I'd never learnt music in a traditional way. I just started playing guitar and writing things that sounded good to me.

All those years of listening had gone somewhere.

When I started making it, the relationship changed.

Being in a band was exciting. There was something about bringing an idea to other people and watching it change when they started playing it and adding something of themselves. You'd turn up every week, play the same songs, argue about things, find something new by accident.

There was an alchemy to it that you can't really recreate on your own.

But making music also meant seeing the other side.

Lugging an amp and a guitar across London on the Tube to play to a room of 5 people, including the other band on the bill.

Some of the magic goes with it.

You start to understand how the sausage is made and hear things you didn't before.

There are still producers whose music I can't work out at all. I don't know how they made it or why it sounds the way it does. That still feels magical to me.

As I've got older, the role music plays in my life has changed.

It isn't the centre of my identity in the way it once was. I don't spend every night surrounded by people who are into the same things. I don't have the same amount of time to go looking for new music.

But I still get that feeling that I should be making something.

Even when I'm not particularly interested in working on tunes, there's a part of my brain telling me I should be putting all those years of experience to use.

Making something now feels different.

I know what I like. I know what I'm trying to find. I know that constantly making things doesn't necessarily mean making better things.

And when it comes together, there's not much that beats it.
