# Rituals

When I was younger, chaos was my comfort zone. I think I'd grown used to things feeling that way. I lived most of my late teens and twenties chasing excess, believing more was better. If there was a limit, I usually tried to push it as far as it could go.

Over time, I realised the hard part wasn't giving those things up. It was accepting that they weren't made for someone like me. I had to learn something about my own nature. Some people can enjoy things in moderation. I'm not one of them. Once the switch was on, I couldn't turn it off.

For a long time, I thought limitations were things you imposed on yourself. Something to endure because you thought that's what you were supposed to do.

Eventually, I started to understand that some limitations weren't things I was forcing on myself at all. They were things I needed.

Before Quiet Ruin, I was writing songs in my bedroom in London with my Epiphone Casino, a Vox amp and a loop pedal. I'd sit on the bed with the lights off, apart from a spool of fairy lights in the corner and the blinking BPM counter on the pedal. I'd lay down a guitar phrase, loop it and keep adding melodies until something decided to show itself.

Sometimes I'd get completely lost in a loop for hours. Other times, the next layer would bring out a hook I hadn't realised was there.

By the time I took those ideas into a rehearsal room, the songs mostly already existed. Years later I moved to a DAW, but very little changed. The tools became more advanced, but the process is pretty much the same.

I don't think I set out to work that way. I think I gradually figured out that it suited me.

I still use that same Epiphone guitar. The same handful of plugins. One synth. One mic. I know what they'll do, and I know roughly where they can take me. I've learnt that anything else just gets in the way.

Every day I try to do something, even if it's small, that moves the project forward.

Sometimes that's playing the acoustic and working through ideas. Sometimes it's writing something like this or working on the website. The project has gradually become bigger than just the songs.

I admire producers who can be recognised within a couple of seconds of hitting play, even when they're working with the same equipment as everyone else.

Most of the limits I've figured out haven't been ones other people placed on me. You figure them out yourself slowly and often reluctantly.

It takes a while to see them. You have to live long enough to notice the patterns.
