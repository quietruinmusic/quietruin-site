# Cycles

Most of my tracks start on the guitar.

Usually I'll be sitting with the acoustic, or with my Epiphone Casino plugged into the amp, playing around until something clicks. It might be a riff or a set of chords. If a vocal melody starts to show itself naturally, I know I'm onto something.

You can have a decent set of chords and still get nowhere. If I can't hear a vocal melody somewhere in there, it can be tough to know where to take it.

When something does feel right, I'll usually work on it away from the laptop first. I like to get the foundations in place before opening Ableton. A basic structure. The vocal melody. Sometimes the words are complete nonsense at that point. Just sounds in roughly the right syllables.

The lyrics come later.

I'll listen to the instrumental on headphones the next day and gradually figure out what works.

Once I've laid the foundations, I can start putting the meat on the bones.

That's when I start finding out what the song actually is.

I had the original guitar idea for Criminal Soul for a few years. On its own, it was fairly soft and understated. When I eventually got it into the DAW, chopped up a drum sample and found the right kick and snare, it became something else entirely.

Suddenly there was a depth and intensity in it I hadn't expected.

I still get a buzz when that happens.

The right drums can completely change the way a guitar part feels. Something you wrote sitting on the edge of the bed with an acoustic can suddenly sound bigger, darker or more physical than you imagined.

Then comes the detail.

A lot of the time I'll only spend twenty minutes on it.

I'll listen through from the beginning, see if everything still feels right, then make the changes I've been thinking about since the previous session. Maybe I'll add something, move things around or take something away. Then I'll listen again and leave it until the next one.

I've found that works better for me than spending eight hours at the laptop, banging my head against the wall.

A song tends to carry on working itself out in the background. I'll be driving to work or doing something else and it will still be there, like an earworm, except it's your own song.

Sometimes I come back knowing exactly what needs changing.

Other times I just know something is wrong.

I've spent hours moving bass notes around in a song, trying to find the right place for them. On October, one octave felt too cheesy. Another lost the drive and the low end. I kept moving things around until eventually it clicked.

Then there are the microscopic details.

Playing the same part again and again because you can't quite get the recording right. Cutting out clicks in a vocal with tiny edits. Hearing a hard plosive on a word that probably nobody else would notice, but knowing it will bother you forever if you leave it there.

I'm annoyingly attuned to things like that.

I hear them in other people's music now as well.

But not every problem is that small.

Sometimes you know you're trying to force a square peg into a round hole.

You can spend a while pretending otherwise, moving things around and trying another version, but eventually you have to admit the idea just isn't good enough.

I used to find that harder.

There was probably a fear that if I let one go, nothing else would come along. Now I've got better at parking things and accepting that ideas don't arrive in a straight line.

When I finally get a song to the point where the structure is right, the lyrics are right and, most importantly, the mood feels right, I'm usually happy to leave it there.

Eventually, though, there is nothing left to do.

The song is finished.

For a while, there's relief.

Then there's a slight emptiness.

Now what?

And at some point, usually without warning, I'll be sitting with a guitar again and a little idea will appear.

And around again we go.
